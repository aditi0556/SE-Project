SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT LIFE CYCLE (SDLC) — MODEL SELECTION GUIDE
Source: Topic-2 Selection of an SDLC (Mahendra Pratap Singh, Dept. of CSE, NITK)

================================================================
HOW TO CHOOSE A MODEL
================================================================
Selection is driven by four project characteristic categories:
 1. Requirements (clarity, stability, need to demonstrate them)
 2. Project Team (familiarity with domain/technology/tools, turnover, training)
 3. User Community (availability, expertise, desired involvement)
 4. Project Type & Risk (new direction, integration, funding stability, expected longevity)

Six models are compared: Waterfall, V-Shaped, Prototype, Spiral, RAD, Incremental.

================================================================
1. WATERFALL MODEL
================================================================
Use when:
 - Requirements are well known, clearly defined, and can be locked in early.
 - Requirements are NOT expected to change during the project.
 - There is no need to demonstrate requirements or build a proof of concept first.
 - The project is NOT a system integration or enhancement effort (i.e., mostly new,
   self-contained development) and does NOT represent a new product direction.
 - Funding is expected to be stable for the whole life cycle.
 - The product is expected to have a long life in the organization.
Team/User fit:
 - Best when the team is relatively new to the technology/tools (structured, sequential
   phases give time to learn) but the requirements themselves are solid.
 - User representatives may have limited availability — heavy user involvement isn't required
   once requirements are signed off.
Bottom line: Choose Waterfall for well-understood, stable, low-ambiguity projects where
you can define everything up front and don't expect change.

================================================================
2. V-SHAPED MODEL (Verification & Validation)
================================================================
Use when:
 - Requirements are well known and can be defined early, and are unlikely to change.
 - The project involves system integration or is an enhancement to an existing system.
 - Funding is expected to be stable, and the product is expected to have a long life.
 - Training is available for the team if needed.
Team/User fit:
 - Suitable when the team is new to the technology or tools, since built-in
   verification/validation at each stage reduces risk from that inexperience.
 - Like Waterfall, doesn't require heavy ongoing user involvement.
Bottom line: Choose V-Shaped like Waterfall, but specifically when strong testing/validation
discipline is needed — e.g., integration or enhancement work where correctness matters.

================================================================
3. PROTOTYPE MODEL
================================================================
Use when:
 - Requirements are NOT well known or easily defined, and are likely to change.
 - There is a need to demonstrate requirements, or build a proof of concept, to nail
   down what's actually needed.
 - The project may represent a new product direction or a system integration effort.
Team/User fit:
 - Good when the team is new to the problem domain (a prototype helps everyone learn),
   but works best when the team already knows the technology/tools.
 - Ideal when users are new to defining the system themselves but ARE experts in the
   problem domain, and want to be closely involved across all phases.
Bottom line: Choose Prototype when you need to explore and validate unclear requirements
with active, expert user feedback before committing to a full build.

================================================================
4. SPIRAL MODEL
================================================================
Use when:
 - Requirements are poorly understood and expected to change frequently.
 - A strong need exists to demonstrate requirements and prove feasibility (proof of concept).
 - The project is high-risk: possibly a new product direction, a system integration
   project, with funding that may NOT be stable throughout the life cycle.
 - The product is expected to have a long organizational life, justifying the
   extra risk-analysis overhead of repeated spiral iterations.
Team/User fit:
 - Suited to teams new to the problem domain, the technology, AND the tools — and where
   team members may be reassigned mid-project.
 - Works when user availability is limited and users are new to system definition.
Bottom line: Choose Spiral for large, high-risk, high-uncertainty projects where
iterative risk analysis is essential and unstable funding/requirements are expected.

================================================================
5. RAD (RAPID APPLICATION DEVELOPMENT) MODEL
================================================================
Use when:
 - Requirements are well known, can be defined early, and are stable — but still
   benefit from demonstration and a proof of concept for stakeholder buy-in.
 - The project is a system integration effort or an enhancement to an existing system.
 - Funding is expected to be stable.
Team/User fit:
 - Best when the team already knows the problem domain, technology, and tools well
   (RAD relies on speed, not learning-on-the-job), and won't be reassigned mid-project.
 - Training is available if needed.
 - Requires highly available, expert users who want to be closely involved throughout.
Bottom line: Choose RAD when you have clear requirements, an experienced team, and
engaged expert users, and you want to move fast — typically for integration/enhancement
projects with stable funding.

================================================================
6. INCREMENTAL MODEL
================================================================
Use when:
 - Requirements can be defined early overall, even if some details evolve — but are
   not expected to change drastically once each increment is locked in.
 - The project could represent a new product direction, a system integration effort,
   or an enhancement to an existing system.
 - The product is expected to have a long life in the organization.
Team/User fit:
 - Good fit when the team is new to the technology/tools and may face reassignment
   during the life cycle — training should be available.
 - Works when user availability is limited/restricted, and users are new to system
   definition but are domain experts (though they don't need to be involved in
   every single phase, unlike Prototype/RAD).
Bottom line: Choose Incremental when you want to deliver working pieces of the system
progressively, tolerate some team turnover, and don't need constant full-time user
involvement.
